**Nobody taught me this.** Water left in an outdoor faucet line freezes, expands, and splits the
pipe, and you often don't find out until spring when it floods the basement. Winterizing takes
about 15 minutes per faucet.

## Step-by-step

1. **Disconnect the hose.** Drain it and store it inside or in a shed.

   ![Outdoor hose bib on a house wall with a garden hose still attached]({{ '/assets/images/how-to-winterize-an-outdoor-faucet/step01-hose-bib-with-hose.jpg' | relative_url }}){: loading="lazy"}
   <small class="photo-credit">Photo: [HelpAtMyHome](https://www.flickr.com/photos/195752343@N05/52119792040/), [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/), via Flickr.</small>

2. **Find the indoor shutoff valve** for that faucet. Go inside and look on the pipe right where it
   passes through the wall, usually in the basement ceiling, crawlspace, or utility room. {% comment %}SHOT LIST (original photo needed): Basement/crawlspace view of the supply pipe heading to the exterior wall with the shutoff valve on it, wall and pipe path visible so readers can follow it.{% endcomment %}
3. **Close the indoor valve.** Lever handles turn a quarter turn; round handles turn clockwise until
   snug. Don't force it.

   ![Round red-handled gate valve on a copper water pipe indoors]({{ '/assets/images/how-to-winterize-an-outdoor-faucet/step03-round-handle-shutoff-valve.jpg' | relative_url }}){: loading="lazy"}
   <small class="photo-credit">Photo: [Mariegriffiths (English Wikipedia)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:GateValve.JPG), [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/), via Wikimedia Commons; resized.</small>

4. **Open the outdoor faucet fully.** Water will run out and then stop.
5. **Open the bleeder cap** on the indoor valve, if it has one (a small cap on the side). Hold a
   bucket under it. Air rushes in and the rest of the water drains out the faucet outside. {% comment %}SHOT LIST (original photo needed): Close-up of a stop-and-waste valve's small bleeder cap being unscrewed, bucket/cup under it catching drips.{% endcomment %}
6. **Close the bleeder cap** once it stops dripping.
7. **Decide on the outdoor faucet position.** The Red Cross recommends leaving it open so leftover
   water can expand without breaking the pipe.
8. **Frost-free faucet?** You may not have an indoor valve. Just make sure the hose is off so the
   body can drain. If it has a manual vacuum breaker on the spout, hold the drain tab open until it
   stops dripping. {% comment %}SHOT LIST (original photo needed): Side-by-side: a frost-free sillcock (long body through the rim joist, seen from inside) vs. a standard hose bib with a short body.{% endcomment %}
9. **Insulate.** Put an {% include aff-link.html text="insulated faucet cover" url="AFFILIATE_LINK_PLACEHOLDER" retailer="Amazon" %}
   over the spigot, and wrap any exposed pipe in unheated spaces with foam insulation. {% comment %}SHOT LIST (original photo needed): Insulated foam faucet cover installed snugly over the hose bib on siding.{% endcomment %}
10. **Repeat for every outdoor faucet.**
11. **In spring,** close the bleeder and **close the outdoor faucet first**, then open the indoor
    valve slowly. (If the outdoor faucet is still open, water sprays out the moment you open the
    indoor valve.) Check outside and inside for leaks before hooking the hose back up.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the hose connected to a frost-free faucet
- Closing the indoor valve but not opening the outside faucet to drain
- Forgetting the bleeder cap, so water stays trapped in the line
- Missing a second faucet on the other side of the house
